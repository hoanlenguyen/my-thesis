## Slide 1. Title (about 0:35, 71 words)

Hello, my name is Hoan Le. This is my master's thesis in Information Processing Science at the University of Oulu, supervised by Yueqiang Xu. The thesis asks whether language model agents can give households reliable, cost-saving advice on when to run their appliances under dynamic electricity prices. I will keep the background short and spend most of the time on what I found, how I found it, and a live demonstration.

## Slide 2. Motivation (about 1:00, 135 words)

In Finland, households on spot-price contracts have paid a price that changes every fifteen minutes since October 2025. Prices within a day vary a lot: from 2023 to 2026, the most expensive hour of a day cost a median of 3.1 times the cheapest, and one day in five had at least one hour with a negative price. So running the dishwasher or the washing machine at the right time saves money. Doing it by hand is hard, though, when several appliances must fit the price curve and the household must also stay under a power limit. Network companies may now charge for power above 8 kilowatts, and a home with an electric car and a sauna can come close to it. Could an AI assistant plan this every day, for anyone, in plain language?

## Slide 3. Previous research and research questions (about 1:25, 186 words)

Language models have recently been tried for exactly this. In the first home energy management systems built on large language models, agents schedule the appliances. But the published systems have clear limits. In the hierarchical multi-agent system of El Makroum and colleagues, only one of three models coordinated all appliances, and the evaluation covered a single day. Jonah and colleagues list coordination failures, untested constraint safety and short evaluations as open problems, and recommend that every schedule be checked before it runs. It is also unclear what an agent adds over tools households can already install, such as a cheapest-hours automation in Home Assistant or the open-source optimiser EMHASS. This leads to my research question: how can a hierarchical ReAct multi-agent system be designed to give households reliable, cost-saving daily recommendations? Sub-question one asks what the recommendations save and cost compared with a base language model without tools. Sub-question two asks under which price patterns and household loads they save at least as much as existing tools. And sub-question three asks whether dividing the work between two agents helps at all, compared with a single agent.

## Slide 4. Research method (about 0:45, 95 words)

I followed Design Science Research: build an artifact, evaluate it, and learn from each version. The design went through fourteen iterations. It started with four agents: an orchestrator, a profiler, a scheduler and a conflict resolver. Each iteration fixed a problem I saw in the traces, such as a missing appliance, a start outside its window, or slow model calls made one after the other. Two lessons shaped the final design: let a solver do the search, and move fixed procedures out of the agents into deterministic code. That reduced the system to two agents.

## Slide 5. The artifact (about 1:20, 171 words)

This is the final artifact, built with LangGraph. A household's plan for the day goes in: which appliances to run, when each is ready, and when it must finish. The Orchestrator agent reads the plan and briefs the Scheduler. Around the agents sits a deterministic harness, shown in grey, with no language model in it. It profiles the appliances from the household data and retrieves two similar solved days as worked examples. The Scheduler agent works in a ReAct loop of thought, action and observation. It reads the day's prices and calls an optimiser tool, a mixed-integer program that finds the cheapest start times within the windows and the 8 kilowatt limit. The harness then verifies the schedule: every appliance present, every start inside its window, the peak under the limit. If a check fails, the problem goes back to the Scheduler for one more round. Finally, the Orchestrator explains the result to the household. For comparison, I also built a single agent that does both roles inside the same harness.

## Slide 6. Data and evaluation (about 1:10, 147 words)

To evaluate the design I needed realistic days. The appliance cycles come from the REFIT dataset: real washing-machine, dishwasher and tumble-dryer cycles measured in eighteen UK homes. The prices are real Finnish Nord Pool prices at fifteen-minute resolution. Each day combines one of four price patterns, stable, volatile, a price spike or negative prices, with one of three household loads: light, heavy, and electrified, which adds an electric car charge and a 6.8 kilowatt sauna. Before running anything, I pre-registered nine hypotheses and their tests, and I kept a held-out set of 72 days, called Q3, that was never used during design. Every system ran three times per day with GPT-6 Luna, and every schedule was scored by the same function, against no scheduling and against the exact optimum. The comparison includes the language model without tools, a cheapest-window rule, the Home Assistant cheapest-hours automation, and EMHASS.

## Slide 7. Result 1: reliability and saving (about 1:45, 225 words)

Here is the main result. The left chart shows the saving against starting every appliance as soon as it is ready, pooled over the 72 held-out days. The right chart shows how often the schedule was valid. The optimum saves 15.92 percent. The Multi-Agent Pipeline also saves 15.92 percent: it found the optimal schedule on every day, and all 216 runs were valid. The single agent in the same harness did exactly the same. Now look at the language model on its own, given all the same data in one prompt but no tools. It saves only 4.07 percent, and almost one in four of its schedules is invalid, because it breaks a window or the power limit. It is the same model in both cases. The difference is the design around it: the optimiser tool does the search, and the harness checks every answer. The gap is clearest on the eleven days where the appliances cannot all run in the cheapest slots without breaking the power limit, so they have to be staggered. There, the language model alone found a valid plan in 70 percent of runs, the cheapest-window rule in only 27 percent, and the pipeline in every run. Coordinating several appliances is exactly the failure that earlier studies reported, and here it disappears. This supports hypotheses H1a and H1b, with large effects.

## Slide 8. Result 2: speed and cost (about 1:05, 137 words)

The second part of sub-question one is cost. A recommendation from the pipeline takes 7.4 seconds on average and costs about four hundredths of a cent in API fees. My objectives were under 60 seconds and under ten cents, so both are met by a wide margin. The single agent is even faster and cheaper: 5.0 seconds and two and a half hundredths of a cent. I also ran both designs on a small open model, gpt-oss-20b, on my own laptop, on the exploratory benchmark. It too was valid and optimal in every run, only slower, with a median of 22 to 34 seconds. It often answered in prose first, and the harness's recovery step caught that every time. So the design does not depend on one commercial model, and it could run privately inside a home.

## Slide 9. Result 3: against existing tools (about 1:20, 173 words)

Sub-question two compares the pipeline with tools households already have, split by household load. The green bars are the pipeline; EMHASS and the optimum reach the same height. The rule-based tools fall behind, and the gap grows with the load. On light days the cheapest-hours automation saves 11.1 percent against 15.7. On electrified days, with the car and the sauna, it saves only 1.6 percent, because it plans each appliance on its own and stacks them in the same cheap slots. That pushes the house over 8 kilowatts: only 54 percent of its plans on those days were valid, and only 71 percent for the cheapest window. Over all days the pipeline saved 4.6 percentage points more than the cheapest window and 6.0 more than cheapest hours, and it matched EMHASS within two thousandths of a point. EMHASS is an optimiser too, so this is the expected ceiling. The difference is that EMHASS needs a configured installation and returns a power plan, while the pipeline takes a plain description and explains its answer.

## Slide 10. Result 4: pre-registered tests (about 0:50, 104 words)

Here are all nine pre-registered tests, analysed exactly as registered. Seven are supported. The pipeline beats the language model alone on saving and on validity, meets both cost objectives, beats both Home Assistant rules, and is equivalent to EMHASS. Two are not supported. I expected the advantage over the rules to be larger on electrified days. The averages point that way, 6.7 and 2.4 points, but the differences between days vary a lot, and the tests were not significant, with p-values of 0.30 and 0.51. I report them as they are: the advantage is large under every condition, not only on the heaviest days.

## Slide 11. Result 5: what makes it work (about 1:40, 220 words)

This brings me to sub-question three: does dividing the work between two agents help? The single agent in the same harness returned the same schedules in every run, but it was faster and cheaper, and the differences were significant. The second agent's brief only restated what the harness already provided, and on the held-out days no run needed the feedback round. This comparison was not pre-registered, so I report it descriptively, but it is clear-cut: the two designs never differed in a single schedule. So the reliability did not come from having two agents. It came from the harness and the tools. From the iterations I drew six design principles, and the four most important are on this slide. Numbers and search come from deterministic tools and a solver. Every schedule is verified by code, not by an agent. Fixed procedures belong in the harness. And a second agent is added only when it has decisions of its own. Each of these came from a failure I saw. For example, early versions let the agents move appliances one at a time; they often settled on a valid but expensive plan, and no amount of prompting fixed that. Handing the search to a solver did. The hierarchy may still pay off with less structured requests, which this study did not test.

The harness profiles the appliances and retrieves similar solved days. The Scheduler writes a thought, reads the prices, and calls the optimiser with all three appliances. The verification step checks the schedule, and it passes. The Orchestrator then explains the plan in plain language. In this run, the washing machine and the dishwasher start at 19:45 and the dryer at 20:30. Next to it, the single agent runs in the same harness and gives the same schedule. Finally, the household pages show each REFIT home, its appliances and its estimated saving over its whole recording period at today's prices.

## Slide 13. Significance and limitations (about 1:20, 176 words)

What does this mean? First, a language model on its own is not a reliable scheduler, but the same model inside the right design is: valid in every run, optimal, and cheap. Second, the agent's value is not better optimisation, since EMHASS matches it. Its value is access: a household describes its day in plain terms and gets an explained, verified plan without configuring an optimiser. Third, the design principles are a reusable pattern for agentic systems that must respect hard constraints. There are limitations. The appliance data come from UK homes and the prices from Finland. The households are simulated, with no field trial. The requests are structured, not free conversation. The 8 kilowatt threshold is applied as a hard limit per 15-minute slot. And in euros the savings are modest: about 16 percent of what the flexible appliances cost, which for the household with the most to gain is about 35 euros a year at today's prices. The value grows with larger flexible loads, such as the car, which is where the rules fail.

## Slide 14. Conclusion (about 0:50, 104 words)

To conclude. The research question asked how to design such a system. My answer: let the agents decide and explain, let a solver search, and let code verify. Sub-question one: the recommendations are valid and optimal, in under ten seconds, for a fraction of a cent. Sub-question two: they beat the Home Assistant rules under every price pattern and load, and they equal EMHASS. Sub-question three: a second agent adds time and cost but no reliability; on these problems one agent is enough. Future work includes a field trial with real households, requests in free text, and smaller local models. Thank you for watching.
