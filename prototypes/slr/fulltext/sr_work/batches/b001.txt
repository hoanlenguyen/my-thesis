TI: What LLM-Simulated Users Can and Cannot Tell Us About Conversational Energy Management Systems
AB: Conversational Home Energy Management Systems (HEMS), capable of understanding and responding to natural language commands on home energy
    management (HEM) tasks, introduce new evaluation challenges since users can request any information or action in free-form language. Consequently,
    the Large Language Model (LLM)-as-simulated-user methodology, where LLMs role-play as users to evaluate conversational systems, is emerging in
    HEMS research. However, growing evidence from the Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) community indicates that LLM-simulated users are unreliable
    proxies for real humans - they are systematically more cooperative, inflate system success rates, and fail to reproduce demographic-specific
    interaction patterns. This study reframes the value of the LLM-as-simulated-user methodology for HEMS research by hypothesizing that it can still
    function as a system diagnostic tool. To test this hypothesis, this study conducts the first empirical assessment of the LLM-as-simulated-user
    methodology in the HEMS domain, analyzing 105 conversational logs between a multi-agent HEMS and LLM-simulated users (three user personas × seven
    HEM scenarios × five repetitions). This analysis assesses this evaluation methodology across multiple validity dimensions, including
    reproducibility, scenario discrimination, diagnostic utility, simulated user cooperativeness and persona fidelity, and deployability. Results show
    that this methodology produces reproducible pass/fail verdicts (90.5% goal-outcome consistency across repeated trials), discriminates system
    capability boundaries across task complexity levels, and surfaces actionable defects including factual errors and interaction weaknesses. However,
    consistent with prior literature, simulated users exhibit politeness in 96% of conversations (vs. 20% for real humans) and show weak persona
    fidelity (1.8 of 4 defined behaviors present on average, validated by human annotation with Cohen's κ = 0.83). These findings provide
    evidence-based guidance for HEMS researchers on the appropriate use of the LLM-as-simulated-user methodology and contribute to the broader
    discourse on LLM evaluation validity in HCI in domain-specific applications.  © 2026 Copyright held by the owner/author(s).

TI: What Breaks Under Pruning in Smart Homes, and When? Evaluating LLM Degradation Across Architectures and Task Complexity
AB: Pruning can reduce the deployment cost of large language models (LLMs), but its impact on context-grounded tool calling remains poorly understood.
    We systematically study pruning-induced degradation in smart-home tool calling across four LLMs spanning dense Transformer, dense hybrid, and
    mixture-of-experts (MoE) architectures, together with depth, width, hybrid, and expert pruning methods. After post-pruning supervised fine-tuning
    (SFT), we evaluate more than 19,500 instances from three smart-home datasets. Beyond aggregate task accuracy, we characterize degradation along
    two dimensions: action components (i.e., operation, device, argument, and value) and task complexity. Our results show that dense models have
    narrow safe pruning regions followed by sharp degradation, while MoE models tolerate substantially more pruning. Pruning degrades grounded
    specificity before schema-level intent, and aggressive dense pruning can induce systematic over-refusal. These findings highlight the importance
    of evaluating pruning beyond aggregate accuracy when selecting pruned LLMs for reliable tool execution.
